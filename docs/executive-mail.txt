I’ve been considering how these learnings could be leveraged to support MNM’s broader advocacy and communications efforts. Below are some initial thoughts that may also be useful for our other teams.

The big picture: Zambia is not a country where malaria is simply being fought with standard tools. It is a country where the standard tools have already been deployed at scale, where the gains are plateauing despite that deployment, and where the next decade's determinants like climate, resistance, vaccine confounding, financing graduation, demographic transition, etc. are all arriving simultaneously. Whatever happens in Zambia in the next five years will foreshadow what happens across high-burden Africa in the ten years that follow.

Insight 1: The curve has bent the wrong way, and nobody is saying it loudly enough: Reported malaria incidence has increased in recent years. The numbers are there in WMR. But, what is really important is that this is not a data artefact; it is a signal that the current intervention package has hit a ceiling. The global malaria narrative is still organised around "scale what works." Zambia is quietly telling us that scaling what works is no longer enough. There is an opportunity to reframe the global conversation from scale to reinvention and to do it with a country whose data is credible enough that the reframing will stick.

Insight 2: Zambia is the best case in Africa for a "platform country” strategy: Most malaria advocacy still treats countries as recipients of vertical programmes. Zambia has the ingredients to be treated instead as a platform: a statutory public health institute with real authority (ZNPHI under the 2020 Act) which has access to all surveillance data, a sophisticated sub-national data system for malaria (MRRS with 111/116 district coverage), a credible national elimination centre (NMEC), a funded data integration backbone (PHISMS), a working One Health governance structure, and a climate-health catalytic fund already in the door. No other high-burden African country has all of these at once. It is possible to make Zambia the explicit global "reference platform" for next-generation malaria control, and use that designation to attract R&D, tool evaluation, and private-sector partnerships that would otherwise go to half a dozen countries in parallel.

Insight 3: The financing graduation story is the most important malaria advocacy story nobody is telling: Zambia's income trajectory and its malaria trajectory are pulling apart, and the global financing architecture has no answer for it. Zambia moved into lower-middle-income status in 2011, and then in 2022 the World Bank reclassified it back down to low-income. Looking forward, Zambia has a credible path to leapfrog toward economic prosperity– critical minerals, regional power, agricultural transformation, a young workforce and addressing malaria is part of what unlocks that path. But that same upward trajectory creates a structural risk: as GNI per capita rises, eligibility for concessional flows narrows at exactly the moment climate, insecticide and drug resistance, and demographic pressure are pushing the malaria burden back up. This is the core misalignment. The Global Fund allocation formula, the Gavi co-financing curve, PMI's country tiering, and bilateral graduation rules were all designed for a world in which malaria was steadily declining and income was steadily rising and so "graduation" was safe. That world no longer exists. Zambia is the cleanest early case of a country where the burden curve and the income curve are diverging, and it will not be the last. Kenya, Ghana, Senegal, and Rwanda are on similar tracks. Malaria No More is one of the very few organisations with the standing, the narrative discipline, and the political access to name this publicly without being dismissed as a single-country complaint. No multilateral will say it about itself. No endemic-country government can say it without appearing to lobby against its own graduation. The WHO cannot frame it as a financing-architecture failure. MNM can.

Insight 4: The three converging shocks climate, resistance, and vaccine confounding are not being modelled together anywhere in the world: In Zambia, all three are happening at once: climate-driven vector ecology is shifting (including the counterintuitive drought–funestus pathway), artemisinin and RDT resistance are under active monitoring, and the R21 vaccine rollout is reshaping baseline transmission in 83–88 of 116 districts. No global modelling exercise has integrated all three drivers into a single forward view of malaria burden. Whoever builds that integrated model first will own the next decade of malaria planning. Malaria No More could convene a consortium (IMACS, MAP, Imperial, Oxford, Broad) to produce the first integrated climate-resistance-vaccine malaria outlook, using Zambia as the prototype. 

Insight 5: The vaccine rollout is the most important narrative inflection point since ITNs: R21 is going in fast, and the priority threshold of 250+ per 1,000 incidence means the highest-burden districts get vaccinated first. This is the most consequential structural change in African malaria since ITN/ LLIN distribution and the advocacy community is treating it as a routine programme update. We can own a unique narrative. Concretely: (a) fund a Zambia longitudinal cohort that tracks what happens to burden, equity, health-seeking behaviour and community demand as vaccine coverage scales; (b) publish an annual "Vaccine Era" report using Zambia as the reference case; (c) use the data to lobby hard against the inevitable argument that vaccines let donors reduce investment in nets, surveillance and case management or otherwise.

Insight 6: The communications asset at the grassroots: Zambia has 45,000 community based volunteers at a 1:500 ratio and the government is moving forward with the plan of polyvalent CBVs (currently ~11,000) with government stipends and digital reporting. This is, quite literally, a massive frontline workforce on the continent alongwith an ongoing switch in incentive structure. It is a photogenic, ground-truth, community-rooted story that we could elevate into the global malaria narrative. It would reframe “surveillance”, a word that bores donors as “one of the largest neighbourhood health network in Africa," which is the story private donors, corporate partners and the public actually respond to.

Insight 7: Private-sector opportunities that sit outside The Global Fund's architecture: We noted several unglamorous plumbing gaps that The Global Fund's vertical grants structurally cannot fund: the CHW-to-phone ratio (currently 1:6); the LMIS-surveillance bridge to prevent alerts being issued into stockouts; the migration of resistance data out of Excel; the climate-station network at ZMD. Each of these is a natural fit for a private-sector corporate partnership– telcos for devices, logistics companies for LMIS, software companies for database migration, reinsurers and climate-tech firms for ZMD pipes. Malaria No More's unique asset among malaria organisations is the ability to broker these partnerships at the executive level. We can build a "Zambia Corporate Alliance" of four or five anchor partners, each owning one plumbing gap, with MNM as the convening authority.

Insight 8: The One Health opening is a chance to expand malaria's political base: Zambia has one of the few functioning One Health Technical Working Groups in the region with real cross-ministry authority. Malaria advocates have historically treated One Health as a zoonosis concern, but entomology, land use, water management and livestock movement all sit at the One Health table in Lusaka. By folding anopheline vector surveillance and climate-vector modelling into One Health, malaria gains a political footprint in ministries (environment, water, livestock) that are growing in budget and climate relevance, while the traditional health ministry faces fiscal pressure. This is a structural hedge against malaria losing political oxygen inside the Ministry of Health.

Insight 9: The advocacy vacuum on data dignity: A striking pattern in Zambia is that community health workers produce most of the signal and receive almost nothing back– no district-level feedback rhythm, no visible use of their data, no recognition loop. This is both a morale problem and a powerful advocacy story. There is an opportunity to run a "Data Dignity" campaign that makes visible the invisible labour of CBVs and pushes for a mandated feedback loop in every malaria grant. It costs almost nothing. It lands with the public. And it materially improves data quality.

Insight 10: The untapped R&D opportunity: NMEC does have the appetite for next-generation vector control tools and it also has the surveillance backbone to be the continent's lead evaluation site. There is a real chance that Zambia becomes the natural home for the next generation of malaria R&D trials like newer dual active-ingredient nets, spatial repellents, attractive targeted sugar baits, or gene drive. I didn’t probe whether one is already going on though. But, there is an opportunity to convene the likes of IVCC, the Gates Foundation, Target Malaria and others, and position Zambia as the designated continental reference site for next-gen tool evaluation. This is the kind of move that changes where global R&D money flows for the next few years.

Insight 11: The quiet story inside the numbers– resistance is the sleeping giant: 100 molecular resistance monitoring sites exist and the data lives in Excel. Both artemisinin and RDT resistance are being tracked. Nobody is telling the global community what that data adds up to, because it has not been assembled. There is an opportunity to commission an independent "State of Resistance in Zambia" report, co-branded with the likes of WWARN or a similar technical body, and use it to keep resistance on the global agenda at a moment when attention is drifting to vaccines. Resistance is the story that will define the late 2020s and early 2030s, and whoever owns the narrative now will own the response later.

Insight 12: The financing geometry Malaria No More can push for: Inside GC8, Malaria, RSSH are being programmed as separate windows. That geometry made sense for vertical disease control. It does not make sense for climate-informed, surveillance-anchored, community-mobilised malaria response. An advocacy ask would be: programme GC8 Malaria, RSSH as a single portfolio in Zambia as a pilot, and then use the Zambia case to push for portfolio-level programming globally in GC9.

Insight 13: The most important human fact in the mission: The Director General of ZNPHI openly admitted that the institution does not know whether its own data is useful for climate-health analytics and wants help building the framework to answer that question. This kind of candour is rare, and it is a direct invitation. The right response is not a grant or a programme– it is a sustained leadership-level relationship with ZNPHI's leadership. The highest-leverage thing Malaria No More can do in Zambia over the next 24 months may simply be to be the trusted outside voice that a candid, reform-minded DG or a Permanent Secretary calls when he needs an argument made in Washington, Geneva or New York.

The bottom line is, Zambia is the country where the next decade of malaria is being written first, and there is an opportunity for us to help write it.
